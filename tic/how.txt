Saans works in five steps.

1. Sense. Each sewer crew carries a low-cost sensor that measures methane, hydrogen sulphide and oxygen. My PhD work is on gas sensors, and I know why cheap sensors fail in the field. Sewers are humid, and humidity makes low-cost sensors drift and give false readings. Saans uses humidity compensation and simple daily calibration checks so the readings can be trusted. A sensor that gives false alarms gets ignored. A sensor that misses gas kills. Reliability is the whole point.

2. Permit. Before anyone enters a manhole, the supervisor takes a reading at the opening. The device logs the gas levels with GPS location and time on a mobile app. If levels are unsafe, the app blocks the entry permit and suggests ventilation or mechanical cleaning instead.

3. Pay. This is the key change. The city links contractor payments to the Saans log. A work order is only cleared for payment if a safe reading was recorded before entry. Today a contractor saves money by skipping safety. With Saans, skipping safety means not getting paid. The incentive flips without needing more inspectors.

4. Map. Every reading is stored on a city dashboard. Over time it shows which sewer lines have dangerous gas build-up, which are blocked and where hydrogen sulphide may be corroding concrete pipes. The city gets an underground early-warning map at almost no extra cost.

5. Scale. The plan starts small and grows.
- Phase 1: Pilot in one city ward with 50 sewer crews for six months. Measure entries logged, unsafe entries blocked and zero deaths.
- Phase 2: Expand to the full city and link to the NAMASTE worker database so every registered worker is covered.
- Phase 3: Offer Saans to other cities as a ready system. Add fixed sensors at high-risk manholes for continuous monitoring.

Why this can work now:
- The legal duty already exists. Saans helps cities prove they follow it.
- Each death now costs the state Rs 30 lakh in compensation. That money could buy around 66 professional gas detectors. Prevention is cheaper than compensation.
- The technology is proven in labs. What is missing is manufacturing at scale, a reliable digital platform and a partner with a strong safety culture.

The right partner would be an organisation that can build rugged hardware in large volumes, run city-wide digital platforms and already follows a zero-harm safety culture in its own plants. With that support, Saans can move from a lab prototype to every ward in India.

Success will be measured simply: unsafe entries prevented, workers covered and sewer deaths brought to zero.
